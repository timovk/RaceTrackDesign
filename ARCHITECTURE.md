`core` never touches the DOM, so the same code runs in the app, in workers, in tests and in Node scripts.

## Conventions

- **Units.** Metres, seconds and radians. Gradients are fractions (0.05 is 5%).
- **World coordinates.** Metres from the map's top-left corner, x east, y south. Screen and world share orientation, so no axis flip is needed.
- **Signs.** Positive curvature is a right-hand corner; positive gradient is uphill in the direction of travel; negative vertical curvature is a crest. A positive shoelace area means clockwise.
- **Station ranges** (corners, warnings) are inclusive and may wrap past station 0, in which case `end < start`.

The store owns the project (the only persisted state) and recomputes the track, metrics and warnings whenever the design or terrain changes. Lap times, facilities and the licence follow 150 ms after the last change, in a worker (which keeps the heightmap, sent once per terrain); until they arrive the previous laps stay on screen, mapped onto the new stations by their share of the lap. Views subscribe to topics (`track`, `hover`, `selection`, ...) batched per microtask. Panels update their controls in place rather than rebuilding, so a slider being dragged is never replaced.

## Terrain

1. **Noise.** Three broad fBm octaves are domain-warped for irregular landforms; finer octaves add unwarped detail. Warping the fine octaves too shears them into parallel bands, so they stay isotropic. Octaves continue down to about two cells.
2. **Ridges.** Ridged noise adds crests and spurs only on high ground, so valleys stay open.
3. **Valley floors.** Heights are normalised and shaped with a linear-cubic blend that flattens low ground.
4. **Erosion.** Droplet erosion runs on a 512² copy; the change is upsampled onto the full map, and extended to the edges where droplets cannot reach.
5. **Water** is the height below which the requested share of the map lies.

Generation uses only arithmetic, `Math.floor` and `Math.sqrt` (no `pow`, `exp` or trigonometry), so a seed gives the same map on every engine. A 2048² map takes about two seconds.

The base image uses a colour ramp by height above the lowest land (stretched over at least 120 m, so flat maps stay green), rock on steep slopes, hillshade from the north-west with automatic vertical exaggeration, and depth-shaded water. Contours are a separate transparent image so they toggle without re-rendering.

## Track

- **Spline.** A closed centripetal Catmull-Rom spline through the control points; this parameterisation never forms cusps or loops within a segment. It is sampled densely, then resampled to evenly spaced stations (about 2 m).
- **Curvature** is the change in chord heading per metre, smoothed over about 4 m.
- **Width** blends between control points with a smoothstep.
- **Grading.** The terrain profile under the centreline is smoothed coarse to fine, clamped to the cut/fill limit after every pass, then relaxed with a projected diffusion step. The track bridges small bumps where the limit binds instead of copying them, and the limit holds exactly.

## Analysis

- **Corners.** A station enters a corner below 300 m radius and leaves above 450 m. The entry and exit extend while the radius stays under 1 km. Same-direction corners less than 40 m apart merge into one (a double apex), and turns under 10° are dropped. Each corner is classified from its total angle and minimum radius: kink, hairpin, long corner, sweeper, slow or medium. Quick direction changes are then paired as chicanes (tight) or esses (fast). The track is rotated so station 0 is the start line before analysis, so corners are numbered from the start/finish line.
- **Straights** have a radius above 1 km and change direction by no more than 15° in total. The limit stops a long gentle arc from counting as one straight.
- **Warnings.** Per-station tests are grouped into ranges. Crossings, overlaps and too-close stretches come from a spatial grid over the stations, ignoring neighbours along the lap. One issue is reported per stretch, named after the worst problem in it.

## Racing line

Each station may move sideways by an offset *n* along the track normal, staying 1.2 m inside each edge. The line minimises its bending energy Σ θ²/h, where θ is the angle between the chords to the neighbouring stations and h the mean of their lengths: ∫κ² ds measured on the line itself, so moving to the outside of a corner makes it both gentler and longer. Each term depends on three neighbouring offsets, so the exact gradient and Hessian are cheap and the Hessian is a cyclic band five wide. Newton's method solves the problem to convergence:

- the edge limits are kept by a primal-dual interior-point method: a log barrier, weakened from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁹, until the line touches the edges. With no yes-or-no choice per station, the line moves smoothly with the track: moving every control point of a real circuit by up to a centimetre changes an F1 lap by at most 0.03 s;
- the whole loop is solved at once: numbering the stations 0, 1, n−1, 2, n−2, … turns the cyclic matrix into an ordinary band four wide on each side, factorised as LDLᵀ;
- where the Hessian is not positive definite (far from the solution) a multiple of the identity is added, and each step is halved until it lowers the energy plus the barrier;
- on the inside of a sharp bend the line keeps within 90% of the distance at which neighbouring normals cross, where stations would bunch up. It only binds at kinks in the circuit data, such as one at Sochi.

An earlier version linearised the curvature with fixed spacing and took eight steps, cutting the loop on a straight; that model's gradient was not the energy's, so it never converged, and a millimetre's change to a circuit moved an F1 lap by up to 4.6 s. The Newton method takes about 40 steps, 10–20 ms for a 5 km track. Minimum curvature is the standard stand-in for a real line. A direct lap-time optimisation was about 2.4% faster on a synthetic circuit of 90° corners, but too slow to run live; calibration absorbs the difference.

## Lap simulation

A quasi-steady-state point mass on the racing line:

1. **Corner speed** per station by bisection, from tyre grip (falling linearly with load above the car's weight), downforce and vertical curvature (crests unload the tyres, dips load them).
2. **Forward pass**: acceleration limited by power, by traction on the driven wheels after the cornering force is taken (friction ellipse), and for bikes by the wheelie limit. Drag, rolling resistance and gradient act against it.
3. **Backward pass**: braking limited the same way, and for bikes by the stoppie limit.

Both passes start at the slowest corner and run twice round the loop, so the lap closes on itself. The speed is the lowest of the three. Throttle and brake come from the force each speed change needs (full throttle on the rev limiter). Gears are spaced geometrically from first gear to top speed. Cars with an aero range are run at five wing settings and the fastest is kept.

F1 and F2 open DRS in up to three zones, the longest straights of the racing line of at least 400 m. A straight is where the line's radius is above 1 km, and it carries on through a kink that turns the line by less than 5°. The kink is judged by its total turn, which the straights either side fix, not by its peak curvature, which depends on how the line takes it: at Monza the Serraglio kink peaks right at the 1 km limit, and a few centimetres' change to the line used to split its 960 m zone in two (0.2 s on an F1 lap). On the real circuits the slight kinks inside straights turn under 2° and the bends between straights at least 10°. A straight close to 400 m long can still gain or lose its zone when the line changes by a few centimetres, as the corner exits it runs between tighten or open out.

Sectors split the lap of a reference class (GT3) into thirds of time, each line moved to the nearest full-throttle station within 6% of the lap time.

## Calibration

`scripts/raceReference.ts` prints each class's average wheel energy and tyre work per kilometre over all the circuits in `data/circuits`; these are the reference values in `data/racing.json` (see Races).

`scripts/calibrate.ts` builds the flat TUMFTM circuits, runs every class and compares the result with the real qualifying laps in `data/reference-laps.json`. It then fits the parameters described in [docs/CALIBRATION.md](docs/CALIBRATION.md) by golden-section search on squared log errors. `tests/calibration.test.ts` keeps every class within 2.5% RMS and every fitted lap within 5%.

## Facilities

- **Start/finish** (on the track as built, before rotation). Every 5 m is scored on how much of a 202 m grid lies on a straight (radius above 700 m), the distance to the first corner in the FIA sense (at least 45 degrees, radius under 300 m, preferably 250 m away), the grid gradient (at most 2%), the grid width (15 m) and the length of the straight. A hand-placed line snaps to the nearest station.
- **Pit lane.** Parallel candidates start every 20 m on either side, 500 to 660 m long, with 120 m ramps; chord candidates cut straight across the infield and join the track at 25 degrees or less. A candidate is rejected when it leaves the map, enters water, comes within 5 m of another part of the track, needs more than 12 m of earthworks, bends too tightly or has less than 250 m for the boxes; a chord is also rejected when a stop would cost under 8 s (a shortcut). The rest are scored on entry and exit clearance from the racing line, braking at the exit, flatness, gradient, box length and adjacency to the start.
- **Pit loss.** Braking from track speed to the class's limit, the lane at the limit, and accelerating back, against the lap's own time over the same widened stretch.
- **Speed trap:** the end of the longest stretch at the fastest class's top speed. **Overtaking spots:** braking zones with a drop of more than 60 km/h after a full-throttle run of 250 m or more, the four biggest.
- **Marshal posts.** Greedy: from each post, the next goes as far ahead as possible (at most 500 m) while the two posts see each other and every station between them (sampled every 20 m) is seen by one of them. Posts stand 6 m beyond the track edge, on the outside of the next corner or else the inside, with the eye 1.7 m up (at least 1.7 m above the track); a post sees the track where it sees 1 m above the surface, a car's roof. Sight lines are traced every 5 m over the ground as built (`builtGround` of `earthworks.ts`: the road surface, verges and cut and fill banks of the track and pit lane, and the natural terrain beyond), since a graded track runs in its cuttings: traced over the natural terrain, every cutting hid the track behind the hillside it was dug through. When no usual place ahead is in sight (a sharp crest), the search looks every 5 m and also tries posts right behind the verge (3 m out) and on 3 m platforms.
- **Overrides.** Dragging a handle in Analyse saves a world position in the project's `overrides` (start line, pit entry and exit with the side, speed trap), not a station number, so it survives edits that renumber the stations; each is snapped to the nearest station when used. A hand-placed pit lane is built as a parallel lane between its two points and keeps its problems listed instead of being rejected. Overrides are undoable like any edit.

## Licence

`licence.ts` builds a checklist per body from FIA Appendix O 2026 (with supplement 2 for lengths and the maximum number of starters), FIA Appendix H 2026 (marshal posts) and the FIM Standards for Circuits 2024. Each check applies to all grades or to some; the estimated grade is the best one whose required checks all pass. Recommended and info checks are shown but do not block. Each class names the grade it needs in `data/vehicles.json` (`licence`), set by weight/power ratio as in Appendix O.

This is an estimate from the geometry alone: barriers, kerbs, medical centre and buildings are not modelled, and real homologation needs an inspection. The run-off depth and the crest limit (cars keep at least half their weight) are this tool's own assumptions, since the regulations set run-off per circuit.

Run-off is checked per grade with the laps of the classes that need exactly that grade. Each corner gets two escape paths: straight on from the turn-in point, at the fastest speed in the 100 m before it, and along the apex tangent at apex speed. A path starts where it leaves the track surface and runs until it meets water, the map edge or another part of the track (not the stretch within 300 m of the corner). It passes when it is free for the required depth: 30 m at 100 km/h rising to 100 m at 300 km/h for cars, and 1.3 times that (40 to 130 m) for bikes. The ground slope along it is reported against the FIA (25% up, 3% down) and FIM (10% up, 3% down) limits.

## Layouts

A layout is the full circuit with links taken (`layouts.ts`). A link is stored as its two ends (world positions) and its control points; each end snaps to the full circuit's nearest station (within 25 m of the edge), and the link is taken the way round that follows the direction of travel at both ends (a link drawn backwards is turned round). It skips the full circuit's stations between where it leaves and where it joins; the stretches skipped must miss the start line and each other.

**Building.** The layout walks the full circuit (rotated, so station 0 is the start line) from station 0, keeping its stations exactly (position, width, terrain and graded height), and where a link leaves, follows the link: an open centripetal Catmull-Rom spline through the link's points (`sampleOpenSpline`), whose end tangents are the track's heading at both ends, so it leaves and joins without a kink, resampled at the station spacing and widened from the track's width to the link's. The layout's profile is graded with every shared station pinned to the full circuit's height (`gradeProfile` with fixed heights), so the link meets the track at both ends and the rest is untouched. A link leaves and joins at a shallow angle, so for some 40 m at each end its road and verges still overlap the circuit's: there it takes the circuit's surface height (the two are one surface), then eases to its own grading over the grading's smoothing length, or longer where the heights differ more, so the easing adds at most 5% to the gradient. Graded on its own from the shared station, a link on a hillside sat up to a few metres above or below the circuit while they still overlapped, and in 3D the higher road's verge covered the other's asphalt. Heading, curvature, gradient and edges come from the stations as for any track (`finishTrack`). The layout keeps, per station, the full circuit's station it runs on, or -1 on a link. A link that crosses the full circuit away from its ends is a warning, a level crossing.

**Facilities.** A layout shares the full circuit's start line (station 0) and pit lane: `layoutPitLane` maps the pit lane's entry and exit to the layout's stations when the layout keeps every station of the track beside it, else the layout has none. `placeFacilities` takes such a pit lane as given instead of placing one; everything else (grid, speed trap, DRS, overtaking, marshal posts) is placed for the layout, and the licence is assessed for it.

**In the app.** The store builds the full circuit and every layout whenever the design changes. Design edits the full circuit, so there `track` is the full circuit; Analyse and Race show the layout picked, and `track` and everything derived from it (metrics, warnings, lap times, facilities, licence) belong to that layout. Analyses are kept per layout until the design changes, so switching layouts or modes is instant once each is done; for a layout the full circuit is analysed first, for its pit lane. Each layout keeps its own race setup. A race belongs to its layout: it stops when that layout's track or analysis changes or another layout is picked, not when Design shows the full circuit. The map greys out the rest of the circuit round the layout shown; the 3D view builds the skipped stretches of the full circuit and the other layouts' links as plain roads (the ground is shaped for all roads, and run-off stops at them). Link points are kept to the centimetre as they are placed, as the project file stores them, so a layout drawn and the same layout loaded from its file give the same lap times.

## 3D view

`scene3d.ts` builds the geometry in scene coordinates (x east, y up, z south, metres; heights above sea level) and `view3d.ts` shows it with three.js. The 3D view is its own chunk, loaded the first time the map switches to 3D.

**Earthworks.** Roads are centrelines with a height and a half width: the track, and the pit lane (level with the stretch of track beside it, or blending between the track at its ends for a lane across the infield; narrower where it leaves and joins). Around them the ground is shaped as a circuit is built: flat under the road, a 3 m grass verge falling 3%, then an embankment (1 in 2) down to lower ground or a cutting (1 in 1.4) up into higher ground, until it meets the natural terrain. Every road segment within reach puts a floor (its embankment) and a ceiling (its cutting) on the ground at a point, and the natural height is clamped between them; so parts of the track close together share their banks, and where two roads at different heights cannot both be met the ground splits the difference. Under the roads the ground sits 0.3 m below the surface (easing out over the first 2 m of bank), so the road surfaces never fight it for depth. Segments sit in 32 m buckets over the map, each listing the segments that reach it, so a height lookup checks only nearby ones.

**Terrain.** A quadtree of square patches of 32 cells covers the map: 1/256 of the map at the root (32 m on an 8 km map), split while the nearest road (or point of the run-off, sampled every 8 m) is closer than a third of the patch size, down to 2 m cells near the track and under the run-off; the parts of a split patch that are not near a road themselves keep half the detail. Each patch samples the shaped ground with a one-cell border, so its normals match its neighbours'. Patches of different detail leave small cracks between them, which skirts hanging down from every patch edge hide. On Spa the terrain has about 525,000 vertices and builds in about 60 ms; a 13 km track needs about a million vertices.

**Roads.** The paved surface is built as strips across each station (edge asphalt, a white line 0.25 m wide from 0.3 m inside the edge, the coloured middle), so nothing overlaps within it; the verges have a short skirt into the ground. The start line lies on top. Polygon offsets order the overlapping surfaces: start line over track, track over pit lane, both over the verges, all over the ground. The asphalt between the lines takes the flat map's track colouring.

**The model.** The map's sides go down from the ground (or up to the water level where the edge is under water) to a base below the lowest point, as on a model. Water is a translucent plane at the water level.

**Drawing.** The ground takes its colour in the shader: a height ramp with the flat map's zones in deeper, more natural colours (`GROUND_RAMP`), bare rock on steep slopes, grass on embankments and earth in cuttings (from how far the earthworks moved the ground at each vertex), a darker bed under water, and contour lines from the height with screen-space derivatives. Light comes from a sun in the north-west (as the flat map's hillshade) and from the sky (below); haze sits behind. Heights are exaggerated (×3 by default) by scaling the model vertically around its lowest point. Things of a real size are not stretched with it: a mesh can carry an anchor height per vertex (what the vertex stands on, or its own height for foundations reaching into the ground), and the view keeps every vertex its real height above its anchor (`anchoredHeight`). The pit building and pit wall stand on the pit lane, grandstands on their lowest row, kerbs and grid marks on the track, and run-off on the ground; the racing line and the labels keep their real lift over the track. The view draws only when something changed; the orbit camera keeps above the ground, and its near plane follows the distance to what it looks at, for depth precision. Hover and double-click find the ground under the cursor by marching along the view ray through the shaped ground (the same height function as the mesh) and refining the crossing, so no triangles are tested; a point on the track sets the store's hover station, which the profile shares.

**Light.** `sky.ts` shows two high dynamic range panoramas of the open sky (Poly Haven, CC0: partly cloudy, and overcast scaled by 0.62 to sit under it) on a dome round the camera, mixed by the cloud cover and turned so the photographed sun (34.2° round, 47.9° up) stands where the scene's sun shines from (north-west, at the same height). The same dome, its radiance clipped at 24 so the sun's disc is not counted on top of the directional light, is rendered into a prefiltered environment map per tenth of cloud cover when first needed. That map is the scene's environment: every material is physically based (`MeshStandardMaterial` for the ground, trees, buildings, stands, water; the car paint its own) and takes the sky's diffuse light and reflections from it, so there is no hemisphere light. The fog takes the horizon's colour, measured from the photographs. While the photographs load (about 38 MB) a painted gradient sky and a hemisphere light stand in. `shadows.ts` gives the sun cascaded shadow maps (three.js's CSM): four maps of 4096 texels splitting the view, in the practical split, from the near plane to three times the distance to what the camera looks at (at least 600 m), faded into each other, with a normal bias of one and a half texels of each map. The ground, trees, buildings, stands, marshal posts and cars cast them. The cascades are four directional lights, and a lit material takes the right one only once set up for it (else it gets the sun four times), so every lit material in the scene is set up each frame (a no-op once done), its own shader changes kept by chaining the hooks. CSM replaces three.js's `lights_fragment_begin` chunk for every material with a copy that predates three.js 0.186: it leaves out the set-up before the lights (the DFG term and the multiple-scattering compensation), and with it every reflection of the sky and the sun's highlights. `withLights` keeps three.js's chunk and takes only CSM's lights from its copy.

**The picture.** With Graphics on High, `postFx.ts` finishes the frame: the scene is drawn with four-sample antialiasing into a half-float target that keeps its depth; GTAO (three.js) draws the scene's normals and depth in a pass of its own for ambient occlusion over 2.5 m, denoised (normals rebuilt from the picture's depth creased at every triangle of the surfaces drawn over the ground, whose polygon offset steps the depth); the broadcast's long lenses get depth of field (below); bloom picks out what is brighter than 1.6; then AgX tone mapping and a grade: contrast 1.05 and saturation 1.12 in the view (1.08, 1.15 and a vignette on the broadcast), the saturation down by a quarter and the contrast a little at full cloud cover, as a grey day looks flatter. Basic draws the scene straight to the screen, tone mapped, with the sky and the shadows. **Depth of field**: `lensBlur` takes a broadcast camera as a 2/3-inch sensor (8.8 mm high) at f/2.8, the focal length from the field of view; a point at distance z, focused at s, blurs by f² / (N (s − f)) × |1 − s/z| on the sensor, scaled to the picture's height in pixels. The pass gathers 40 samples on a golden-angle spiral as wide as the pixel's blur (at most 22 pixels at 1080 lines), each taken only as far as its own blur reaches, so a sharp car does not bleed into the blurred ground behind it. It runs only when the blur at infinity is over 0.3 pixels: a 4° lens focused 250 m away blurs the far background by about 3 pixels at 1080 lines, a 1.5° lens at 200 m by the most; wide shots and the onboard cameras stay sharp. A High frame at 1920 × 1080 took 7.8 ms on an RX 7800 XT with 20 cars on track: the textures about 1.5 ms of it, the ambient occlusion with its own pass about 2.4.

**Surfaces.** `surfaces.ts` loads photographed textures (Poly Haven, CC0, in public/textures, about 17 MB): asphalt, grass, dirt, rock and gravel of 2 to 2.5 m with their normal maps, and two aerial photographs of grassland, 15 and 90 m. A surface keeps the colour the view gives it and takes the texture as detail: the texture's colour over its mean colour (measured from the image when it loads, in linear values), scaled by an amount and with some or none of its hue, so it adds variation without changing the colour, and the variation fades as the mipmaps reach the mean far away. Each texture is read twice, shifted by a hash that changes every few tiles, and the two blended (Inigo Quilez's "texture repetition", technique 3), so its repeat does not show; `textureGrad` with the unshifted uv's derivatives keeps the mipmaps right across the shifts. The ground is textured triplanar: from above and along both horizontal axes, weighted by the fourth power of the drawn normal, so the drawn steep slopes (heights ×3) are not stretched; grass where the ground is green, dirt in cuttings, on high dry ground and under water, rock on steep slopes, with the two aerial photographs read blurred over the grass for broad variation, and the normal maps per projection through tangent frames from screen-space derivatives. The track, pit lane, other roads and asphalt run-off take the asphalt with broad patches of a different tone; the track is darker along the racing line (rubber) and lighter and dustier off it, from its `wetLine` attribute; kerbs, grid boxes and the start line take the asphalt faintly under their paint; verges take the grass; the run-off takes gravel where its colour is gravel's, in drifts of tone. These are laid from above only, and fade out on steep faces (a verge's skirt into the ground). Water fills the texture's relief as a surface gets wet. Lakes ripple: five waves of 0.6 to 6.3 m within 40 degrees of the wind at deep-water speeds, on the view's clock, faded where they are finer than the pixels, mirroring the sky.

**Scenery.** `scenery.ts` dresses the circuit from the analysis, all in plain geometry (one mesh per kind, vertex colours):

- *Kerbs*, red and white in 2 m blocks, 1.1 m wide and 5 cm proud, wherever the racing line comes within 2 m of an edge at a corner: on the inside through the apex, 44 m on the outside where the line first reaches it after the apex, and the last 34 m on the outside before the turn-in. Runs on the same side that overlap (a chicane) merge.
- *Run-off* outside each corner, from 30 m before it to 30 m after, tapering at the ends. Its depth is three quarters of the deepest escape path the licence check traced for the corner (as far as it was free, up to what it needed), stopped short of water, the map edge, other parts of the track and steep ground: natural ground climbing or falling more than 25% over 8 m (the steepest FIA Appendix O 7.6 allows run-off to climb; within the first 8 m, 1 m more than that from the verge's edge), or the bank of a cutting more than 3 m deep. It follows the track's own earthworks, down an embankment and up a shallow cutting's bank; a real circuit would grade it, and the licence check says where. Along the track the depth changes by at most a metre a metre, so where a stretch stops short the area's edge tapers to it. Corners that need 80 m or more get 25 m of asphalt before the gravel. The surface follows the shaped ground 0.3 m above it, with a point every 2 m across as the ground under it is (the terrain takes full detail under the run-off), starting level with the verge's edge and easing down to the ground over its last 2 m; before, the ground showed through between coarse terrain cells.
- The *pit building* behind the boxes, 16 m deep and 8 m high, with a 7 m garage door and a 1.5 m pillar in turn along its front, a roof reaching out over the lane, and the pit wall between lane and track. Its footprint keeps stands and trees off it.
- *Grandstands* of seven stepped rows under a roof: the main one along the first 130 m of the start straight (across from the pits when they are beside it), and up to three at the corners after the biggest braking zones, beyond the run-off. A stand needs dry, fairly level ground on the map, clear of every road and footprint; otherwise it is left out.
- A white line at the front of every *grid box*.
- *Marshal posts* where the facilities analysis put them, moved straight back from the track in 2 m steps (up to 120 m) until the pad (4.4 by 3 m) is clear of every road, the run-off and the footprints, as real posts stand behind the run-off: a concrete pad with a small orange-roofed hut at the back, the FIA light panel on a pole at the upstream end facing the oncoming cars, and for a raised post a deck 3 m up on legs with a rail. The flag marshal's rostrum, 2 m up, stands beside the line on the pit side when there is room there, else on the other side. Their footprints keep trees off them.
- *Trees*: `forest` scatters candidates over the terrain from its seed (woods where a slow noise says so, single trees elsewhere, fewer on steep or high ground, none in water, conifers higher up), cached per terrain. `placeTrees` keeps those at least 20 m from every road edge, off the run-off (tested against the nearest station's run-off depth, with a coarse grid that rejects points far from the track at once) and off the footprints, and thins them evenly beyond 40,000. They are drawn as two instanced meshes (conifer and broadleaf), squashed against the relief exaggeration so they keep their real height.

On the 13 km test track the scene has about 3.2 million triangles in 16 draw calls; the scenery builds in about 85 ms on top of the terrain.

**Shots and camera paths.** `shots.ts` places fixed shots in world coordinates: the start from 240 m behind the line and 22 m up; the pit boxes from beyond their exit end, above the pit wall, facing the garages; each numbered corner from a camera tower 15 m beyond its run-off, 16 m above the ground there and at least 12 m above the apex; the steepest climb and drop (the largest gradient over 40 m) from beside the bottom of the slope on its lower side, looking up the road; and the highest point from 150 m out. `clearView` raises a camera in 6 m steps until the ground no longer blocks its line of sight. The view hands the shots and paths its height exaggeration (`display`), so they place their cameras in heights as drawn: a camera tower, the driver's eye or the drone stays its real height above the drawn ground and track, and hills block views as they are drawn. Gradients in the shot names come from the real heights. The view glides between shots, rising over the middle of the move. The hot lap rides the racing line at the time of the selected class's flying lap, at eye height (0.95 m in a car, 1.15 m on a bike), looking at the average of the points 25, 45 and 65 m ahead; the flyover follows the centreline at 60 m/s, 40 m up and 60 m behind, looking 40 to 160 m ahead. Both play in the view's animation loop and can be paused, sped up or stopped.

**Saving images.** The view renders once more at the chosen size (the screen's pixels, twice that, or 3840 × 2160 with the camera's aspect set to 16:9), copies the frame to a 2D canvas, draws the visible labels (and driver codes) on it at the matching scale, and restores the view.

### Cars

**Geometry.** `carMesh.ts` builds car bodies the way a designer would sweep them. A section is half a cross-section, from the bottom centre round the right side to the top centre, made of straight lines and quarter superellipses (rounder or squarer corners); the left half is its mirror. A loft runs sections built by the same calls (so their points match) through stations along the car, denser where the shape changes fast; normals come from the faces round each vertex, and a point where the surface changes (glass, a wheel arch liner, a cockpit) or that is marked hard becomes a crease with its own vertices on each side. A repeated station makes a crease across the body, so a windscreen or a cockpit opening has a sharp edge. Faces orient themselves by their vertex normals. Wings are NACA-style airfoils swept between two leading-edge points; endplates, fins and floors are plates from an outline (ear-clipping triangulation); suspension arms and the halo are tubes and pipes; wheels and helmets are solids of revolution and ellipsoids. Decals are grids laid on a loft by projecting each point from the middle of its section onto the body, lifted 4 mm.

**Models.** `carBodies.ts` describes each car by its real dimensions and side-view, plan-view and section curves (monotone cubic interpolation through key points). Single-seaters have a nose, monocoque and engine cover loft with the airbox and the cockpit opening, sidepods, a floor with the diffuser, front wings of up to four elements, a rear wing with a DRS flap, a halo or an aeroscreen, a helmeted driver and wishbones. Closed cars share one loft whose section has the floor, the wheel arch (its liner rising round the wheel, then a lip out to the body side), the side leaning in to the shoulder, the bonnet or deck, and the greenhouse or canopy, with glass where the windows are; on top of it go the splitter, diffuser, lights, mirrors, the rear wing on swan necks and, for prototypes, the fin; the safety car adds a light bar with an orange beacon each side. Bikes are a fairing, screen, tank and tail with forks, swingarm, exhaust and winglets, ridden by a tucked rider (torso loft, limbs as tapered tubes). Every model comes at three levels of detail (fewer segments, fewer small parts) and builds in a few milliseconds; at full detail a body has 4,000 to 10,000 triangles and a wheel about 2,000.

Every vertex says how it is coloured: trim parts carry their colour and finish (roughness, metalness, clearcoat, glow), painted parts take the livery. Lights also say which light they are (`LAMP`: a headlight, the rear or rain light, either beacon), so the shader can switch them per car; `lampPlaces` finds each light's middle from those vertices (a pair, one each side, when a kind reaches out to the sides), for the glows. Painted vertices have livery coordinates: u along the car (0 at the rear, 1 at the front) and v round the body (0 underneath, 0.25 on the right, 0.5 on top, 0.75 on the left), so a pattern is a function of (u, v) evaluated in the shader with screen-space antialiasing, crisp at any distance and with no texture per car. The DRS flap's vertices carry its hinge, and the vertex shader turns them about it by the per-car DRS opening.

**Liveries.** `raceCars.ts` derives a livery from the team's colour and name: a second colour chosen to stand out (white, black, silver, or a lighter or darker shade), an accent, one of five patterns (a spine stripe, two tones on a diagonal, twin stripes, a sweep up the sides, an arrow) and a variation, all fixed by a hash of the team's name. The decal atlas (`carMaterials.ts`) is drawn on a canvas when a race starts: per car a cell with its number (painted, on a panel, or in a roundel in the class colour) and its team name.

**In the view.** `CarLayer` instances each model per level of detail (body, decals and wheels as instanced meshes, a soft shadow quad per car), with the livery colours, pattern, DRS opening and tyre compound as per-instance attributes. Every frame it places the cars from the race: on the racing line at the interpolated progress and sideways offset (in the pit lane on its path, moved towards the garage while stopped; retired cars where they stopped, turned off the line), on the drawn ground with pitch from the road height under each axle, at real size (the cars sit outside the height-scaled group), the wheels spinning by distance travelled and the front wheels steering by the line's curvature, bikes leaning by speed squared times curvature. A car takes the full model when it is longer than 150 pixels on screen and the medium one above 36, so a car through a long lens keeps its detail. `spreadCars` keeps cars that would overlap side by side: cars within a car length of each other along the lap whose drawn positions would touch move half the overlap apart (the one behind to the side with more room when level), inside the track's edges, easing at 3 m/s of race time. The cars cast and take the sun's shadows with everything else (see **Light**), and the paint reflects the sky's environment map. With 54 cars on track the whole scene took 36 draw calls, not counting the shadow cascades (each draws the casters it covers again).

### Television

**Cameras.** `tvCameras` (in `broadcast.ts`) takes the shot positions of `shots.ts` for the corner towers, behind the grid and over the pit lane, adds one beside every straight of 250 m or more (two thirds along, on the lower side, 8 m up), and then, while a stretch of more than 120 m is out of every camera's sight, another beside its middle (6, 12 or 25 m up, the lowest that sees it), up to 60 cameras. A camera sees a station when the track there is between 12 and 750 m away and the drawn ground stays below the line of sight (`lineOfSight`); every third station is tested, the ones between take the verdict of their neighbours, and gaps under ten stations (about 20 m) are closed so a glimpse lost for a few metres does not end a shot. Positions and heights are as drawn, so the cameras stand on the exaggerated ground and hills block views as they are drawn. Trees are left out of it, so when the broadcast starts the view clears the trees in the cameras' way (`treesInSight` in `scenery.ts`): those within 12 m of a camera, and those whose crown (0.3 of the tree's height wide, plus 2 m) reaches up into the line of sight to a station the camera sees beyond it. The seen stations are binned by bearing round each camera (a quarter of a degree), so a tree is checked only against the lines of sight it could stand in: some 20 ms for ten cameras and 40,000 trees. The trees stay cleared until they are placed again.

**The director.** `Director` keeps its own clock in screen seconds, running only while the race plays, so shots last as long at any playback speed. Each update it keeps the current shot unless it has run its time, a battle has broken up (the car behind more than 1.6 s back, a car between them, or one no longer running; through a pass the battle on screen just swaps its two cars), its car has stopped running, the car is about to leave the camera's sight (`timeInSight` along the coverage at the car's speed, converted to screen time), or an incident happened during it. To cut, it scores candidates: the viewer's car (120 in its battle, 110 alone), incidents (100), battles within a second of the car ahead in the class (60 and up, more for a closer gap and nearer the front), overtakes (62), a stop in the pit box not yet shown (48), the leader (42) and class leaders (32), and three random cars (18 to 28); a subject shown recently loses points, and a little randomness breaks ties. The first candidate a camera can show wins: a trackside camera that keeps its (rear) car in sight at least 3 screen seconds, scored by how long (better when the car is still approaching the camera) and less for the camera just used; or the helicopter, when no camera can or now and then for variety (never twice running). A trackside shot holds 5 to 12 s, cut short about a third of a second before the car leaves the camera's sight; a helicopter shot 6 to 11 s. A battle picked is then followed for 20 screen seconds (`following`, `followed`): every cut in that time shows it again (from the next camera, by the same rules), and the only candidates against it are incidents and replays that may cut in (`cutsIn`: to one of its cars, to the viewer's car, or an incident to a car ahead of it); a replay of the battle itself does not count against the 20 s. The follow ends early when the battle breaks up or the viewer picks a car outside it.

Onboard cameras come before both, now and then: for the viewer's car (35% of the time), a battle (30%), the leaders (25%) and the field (30%), or half the time when no trackside camera can hold the car (a fast playback), never twice running and at most two in four shots, never for an incident or a stop. A battle is shown from the car behind (above the driver, or on the nose) or, a quarter of the time, looking back from the car ahead; a single car from above the driver, the nose or the chase camera. The shot goes with the car (`carrier`) for 6 to 10 s, and ends when it stops running. A car standing in its box is shown from the pit box camera when at least 2 screen seconds of its stop are left (so not at fast playback), until it has left the pit lane, at most 12 s. The first shot within 3 s of the start, at up to 5× speed, is the start from the camera behind the grid, on the first four cars (a `group` subject), for 8 race seconds or until the back of them leaves the camera's sight. A new race starts the director afresh (`reset`).

Replays: an overtake in the top ten and an incident on track (off or contact) are noted with the race time and where the car was. From 2.5 race seconds after one until 25 s after, at up to 5× speed, at most one per 25 screen seconds and only while cars are running, a replay is a candidate (100 for an overtake, 108 for an incident, 125 when it involves the viewer's car), and cuts any shot that has had its 2.5 s, except an incident, the start or a stop. It shows 4 s before to 2 s after an overtake (3 s and 4 s for an incident) at half speed, 12 screen seconds, and is never cut short. Its camera: a third of the time for an overtake, from on board (above the driver of the car that passed, or looking back from the car passed); otherwise the trackside camera, other than the one just used, that sees most of the stretch the cars covered then (at their speed now), or the helicopter when none sees a third of it.

**Replays as drawn.** `ReplayBuffer` (in `race/replay.ts`) keeps every simulation step of the last 40 race seconds, recorded through `RaceController.onStep` while the broadcast is on: per car its progress, speed, sideways offset, status, pit lane progress and whether it is stopped, compound and tyre type, DRS and where it retired, and the safety car. For a past time it gives a `RaceView`: stand-ins for the cars (`Object.create` over the real car, so entrant, class and rules come through) carrying the two recorded steps either side, drawn between them just as the live race is. `CarLayer.update` takes such a view in place of the race, and snaps the cars into place when time jumps back or forward.

**Onboard cameras** are part of each body (`cameras` in `carBodies.ts`, in car coordinates): above the driver (the camera pod on a single-seater's airbox, the front of a closed car's roof, behind and above a rider), on the nose (on top of a single-seater's nose, at a closed car's bonnet, on a bike's fairing), and the first looking back. `CarLayer.mount` turns one into a position, a direction and the car's up from the car's matrix as drawn, so the picture pitches with the road and leans with a bike.

**Framing.** `TvBroadcast` turns shots into camera moves every frame. A trackside camera stands still; it aims a little ahead of its subject (the car or the middle of the battle), panning with the subject's movement and closing what is left with a quarter-second lag, and zooms so the car fills half the free width (a battle three quarters) with `framingFov`, between 1.2° and 45°. The helicopter starts off to one side of the subject's heading, 110 to 190 m up and 170 to 260 m out, turned round the subject until the drawn ground no longer blocks its view; it circles at 2° a second, eases after the subject over a second and a half, and climbs while a hill is in the way. An onboard camera sits on its mount with the car's up, at a fixed angle of view (38 to 42°) and a near plane of 25 cm, so the car's own bodywork stays in the picture without costing depth precision far away. The chase camera follows the racing line 1.3 car lengths plus 3 m behind its car and 2.2 m up (at least 1.5 m over the drawn ground), easing after it in 0.15 s, and frames the car's width at a third of the picture. The pit box camera stands on the pit wall across the fast lane, 4.5 m ahead of the box and 10 m out, 2.4 m up, and pans as the car leaves; the stop timer counts the time standing in the box (`stopStart`, then `record.stationary`) and lists the work done. Before the start (a race at 0 s, paused), the camera walks the grid from tenth to pole, 3 s a car, 5.5 m ahead and 4.5 m to the side away from the pits at head height, with a caption per car, then looks down the grid from the start camera while the start lights come on, one a second from 1.5 s, and go out after 0.4 to 2.4 s more (from the race's seed), and starts the race; a rolling start gets the green flag instead. `RaceController.holdStart`, set while the broadcast is on, makes a new race wait on the grid. A replay is drawn from the buffer at the time `from + (director clock − start) × speed`, behind a sweeping sting into and out of it and a badge while it runs; one no longer kept ends at once. A cut snaps everything. The picture's middle is shifted (`setViewOffset`) into the part of the view the timing tower leaves free. Captions come up on a cut to a new subject, built from the race (`classGap`, `classInterval`, the tyre fitted); pop-ups come from the race's events and from the sector times of the cars on screen.

**Sessions and red flags.** In practice and qualifying a car's interval is no gap on track, so the director sees no battles, and a car in its garage is no pit stop. Cars on a push lap that began at the line (`TvCar.pushing`) are candidates instead (`flying`, captioned "Flying lap"): 50 points, 2 more for each place above twelfth, and 25 more in the last quarter of a qualifying session or its last four minutes. Under a red flag the cars queued in the pit lane are not stops either: the director shows the leader at the head of the queue, and the sector-time pop-ups wait for green. The grid walk and the start lights belong to the race.

**Graphics.** `TvGraphics` draws the rest: a map of the circuit (an SVG in world coordinates, every third station) with a dot per car where it is drawn (the replay's positions during a replay), the cars on screen larger with their codes, the viewer's car ringed and the safety car in yellow; for a live battle once its caption has gone, the gap now and the gap at the line on each of the last five laps both cars finished (from `history[].at`), with the change over the last lap; "Final lap" for 6 s as the leader starts it (or the time runs out); the chequered flag with the winner of each class for 8 s (in a session, the fastest and the time); and the results, ten a class (twenty for one class), once the race is over (in a session its classification by best lap). The timing tower shows the lap and the time left.

### Weather and flags

**Sky and light.** `cloudCover` (in `weatherFx.ts`) reads the race's rain timeline: the rain now, or up to a quarter of an hour ahead (the clouds come first) or ten minutes back, weighted down the further away, through a smoothstep that is fully overcast from 30% rain. `WeatherLayer` turns it into the picture: the photographed sky from partly cloudy to overcast, the sun from 2.8 to 0.35 and greyer, the exposure from 1 to 0.9, the fog the horizon's colour and the grade flatter (see **Light** and **The picture**); in the rain the haze closes in round what the camera looks at (fog from 0.8 times the focus distance plus 150 m out to 4 times plus 1500 m, blended by the rain). Everything takes its light and reflections from the sky's environment map for the cloud cover. Outside a race the weather is fine. With the painted sky (while the photographs load) the gradient goes from clear blue to grey, the sun from 2.2 to 0.3, the hemisphere light from 1.4 to 2.1 and greyer, the exposure a little up and the fog greyer, and the paint and wet track reflect one of three painted environment maps.

**Rain.** Up to 9,000 streaks as line segments in a box in front of the camera, sized to what it looks at (0.6 times the focus distance, 20 to 300 m), fewer in a small box where each is big on screen. The vertex shader places each streak from a fixed random seed, the time and the box's centre so that it stays put in the world and wraps at the box's faces (faded near them); they fall at 9 m/s with a little wind on the view's own clock, which runs at real speed while the race plays, whatever the playback speed.

**Wet surfaces.** The track, pit lane, kerbs, start line, grid marks, run-off and verges use one standard material (`wetSurfaceMaterial`) that darkens (to 45% at most) and turns glossy as the wetness rises (fully from 50%), each surface with its own gloss: asphalt and kerbs fully, run-off half, grass barely. They are lit as everything else (under the painted sky by the sun and the hemisphere light, its map's diffuse light left out), and mirror the sky map more the wetter they are (from 0.35 of its light dry to 0.7 wet, more in a puddle). Track vertices carry their distance from the racing line and from the nearer edge: the 1.5 m either side of the line, where the tyres run, takes the line's wetness (`lineWetness`: 85% of the track's while it rains, and the track's wetness squared once it has stopped, so the line dries first); from 60% wetness, water stands in patches from value noise, more within 3 m of the edges, mirror-smooth and darker. The ground darkens a fifth when wet.

**Spray.** `sprayPuffs` places 64 puffs behind each car from the race clock alone: puff i was thrown up from a rear wheel (alternately left and right) `age` seconds ago, where `age` runs through a cycle of 0.9 s from a phase of its own, so it lies `speed × age × 0.85` back along the racing line (where the car was, carried on a little by its wake), rising to 1 to 2.6 m, spreading sideways and growing from under a metre to five, and fading; its random sizes and heights come from a hash of the car, the puff and the cycle. The same race time always gives the same spray, and a paused race holds it still. The strength is the track's wetness (from 3% to full at 45%) times the speed (from 8 to 50 m/s) times the body (single-seater 1, closed car 0.85, bike 0.35). The puffs are soft camera-facing quads in one instanced mesh, depth-tested without writing depth.

**Lights.** Each car instance carries a set of light bits (in the fourth livery style value). The rear light is on with intermediates or wets (Formula 1's rule for its rain light) and on closed cars in the rain; closed cars run their headlights up in the rain or on a track wetter than 30%; the safety car runs its headlights and flashes its beacons in turn, three times a second, until it comes in this lap, when they go out (F1 Sporting Regulations). The shader brightens a light that is on and darkens a single-seater's rear light that is off. Lights that are on also get an additive glow facing the camera, wider in the wet, dimmer when the light faces away (rear and head lights), drawn at least 4 pixels wide.

**Flags.** `postSignals` (in `flags.ts`) works out every post's flags and board from race control's state, after FIA Appendix H (2026) article 2.5 and the safety car procedures: local yellows through the yellow zone up to the incident and at the post before it (two flags when the zone is a double yellow), green at the first post after the incident; under the safety car, waved yellows and the SC board everywhere until it goes into the pit lane, then waved green; under a virtual safety car or full course yellow, a single yellow and the board everywhere, with the double yellow kept before the incident; green for a lap (the fastest class's race lap) after a neutralisation ends; and blue, while racing, at the next post within 400 m of a car with a car a lap up, or from a class at least 3% faster, within 120 m behind it on track. `lineFlag` gives the chequered flag from the moment the winner finishes, and green for the first 10 s of a rolling start and while the race is released. `FlagLayer` draws three marshals per post (one on the rostrum) at their real size, the first waving the flag, the second a second flag or the board, the third the board when both wave: each flag is the raised arm, a stick and an 80 by 60 cm cloth, swung 0.95 rad either side 1.3 times a second about the axis towards the track, the cloth rippling in the vertex shader; the boards are drawn on a canvas, black on white. The light panels flash twice a second (yellow, blue) or stay lit (green).

**On television.** Race control's messages and the weather pop up with the other events. In the rain a trackside camera has drops on its lens (a fresh set at every cut, more in heavier rain, drawn as blurred discs over the picture, so not in a saved image); the helicopter's lens stays clear.

## Races

### Before the start

A race has one or more classes. `buildRaceModel` builds a model per class; the track, racing line, timing lines, grid slots and pit lane geometry are shared, and everything else (race lap, ratios, fuel, tyres, braking zones, pit speed limit) belongs to the class. For each class it runs the lap simulation a few more times, all at the trim of its qualifying lap:

- the **race lap**, without DRS, as a time per station segment;
- the same lap with 25% less drag (**slipstream**, `air.towDragCut`), with less downforce (**wake**, `air.wakeDownforceLoss`) and with DRS open. Each becomes a per-station ratio to the race lap, so a car's segment time can be blended between them;
- the lap at 75% and at 50% of the dry grip, for a **wet track**. These ratios are blended for any grip level (`gripBlend`: linear between the three laps, extrapolated below 50%), so rain slows the corners and the braking far more than the straights;
- the lap 50 kg heavier, for the lap-time cost of fuel per kg.

Fuel per lap is the class's typical kg/km scaled by this track's wheel energy per km (throttle times power, summed over the lap) against the class's average on the real circuits. Tyre wear is scaled the same way by tyre work, the squared total acceleration summed over distance, clamped to 0.5 to 2 times the reference. Passing zones are the braking onsets with more than 25 km/h lost; their quality grows with the speed lost and the flat-out run before them. The pit lane becomes a path with a speed limit between its ramps and a box per team. The launch curve (speed per metre from a standstill, traction then power limited) serves the start, the pit exit and pulling away from the box or after a neutralisation.

`createField` orders the classes by pace (fastest first; its rules set the start, the flags and the time limit) and draws each class's field from the seed: team pace and reliability, and per driver pace, consistency, tyre management, racecraft, error rate and start reaction. Endurance classes have crews of two or three drivers, fastest first; their cars are known by number. Numbers, driver codes and team names are unique across classes, and every team has its own pit box. `createRaceSetup` takes the field and what the race weekend brought (`WeekendOutcome`: each class's qualifying in grid order, the rubber on the track and each team's belief about its tyre wear). Without qualifying it is worked out from the lap-time model: the best of three laps at the qualifying time by the car's fastest driver, scaled by pace, with scatter and the odd mistake. The grid is class by class. The setup also builds the weather. Without a weekend the track is fully rubbered in and the teams know their tyres, as before the weekend existed.

### Weather

`weather.ts` makes a seeded rain timeline in 10 s steps from an hour before the start: nothing when dry; one or more showers for changeable weather (more in a long race), each building up and easing off with a slow wobble; and for a wet race, rain from before the start that stops during the race six times in ten, sometimes with a later shower. Track wetness (0 dry, 1 standing water) follows the rain towards `rain^0.6`, with a time constant of 5 minutes when getting wetter and 16 minutes when drying.

Per tyre type, wetness sets the grip (a share of a slick's in the dry: slicks fall from 1 to 0.52, intermediates from 0.9 to 0.65, wets from 0.82 to 0.74), an extra loss for aquaplaning on slicks (and on intermediates in standing water), tyre wear (wet tyres overheat and wear up to ten times faster on a drying track; slicks wear less in the wet) and the risk of mistakes (up to twenty-odd times higher on slicks in the wet). Slicks are best up to about 12% wetness, intermediates up to about 65%, then wets; classes without intermediates go straight from slicks to wets at about 28%.

### Stepping

The simulation advances in fixed steps of 0.1 s, so a seed gives the same race at any playback speed. Randomness comes from one seeded generator per car, one for the race and one for race control, and draws use only arithmetic (normal draws sum four uniforms), so every engine agrees.

A car's position is race progress in stations from the start line, negative on the grid. Within a step it walks station by station; each segment takes

    race-lap segment × lap pace × (1 + tow × (towRatio − 1) + wake × (wakeRatio − 1) + DRS) × wet-grip ratio

where lap pace is car and driver pace times (1 + race pace + compound + tyre loss + fuel mass × sensitivity + fuel saving + cold tyres + aquaplaning) times the lap's scatter (larger on a wet track), set at each lap start and after a stop. Yellow flags slow the segments in their zone (6% single, 20% double). Under a neutralisation no car is faster than the VSC delta (the race lap `flags.vscPct` slower), the full course yellow limit (`flags.fcyKmh`) or the safety car delta (25% slower than the race lap), reached over 5 s. Speed caps apply on the launch curve and when braking for the pit entry. Every station crossed is checked against a per-station mark (timing line, sector line, timing loop every 100 m, pit decision point, pit entry, braking zone of any class, DRS detection, telemetry sample, speed trap), and crossings are timed to the exact moment inside the step.

Cars move front to back, starting behind the biggest gap, so each car sees the car ahead already moved. A car stops a minimum gap (6 m + 0.12 s × the speed of the car ahead; 10 m + 0.25 s under a safety car) behind the car ahead unless that car does not block it: off the track, on the pit entry or exit road, being passed, or on the run from the grid to the first braking zone (so the start can shuffle the order). A car that would reach that gap within the step spreads its movement over the whole step, so its speed follows the car ahead instead of surging and stopping. Slipstream is full within 0.3 s of the car ahead and gone at 1 s; the wake is full within 0.4 s and gone at 1.6 s. A standing start launches from zero after each driver's reaction; a rolling start (endurance classes and IndyCar) launches from 100 km/h.

### Racing

At a braking zone of its class, a car within 0.6 s of the car ahead may attack, unless the zone is under yellow, the race is neutralised or the car has not yet crossed the line after a restart. The advantage is the speed ratio at the braking point (the defender's speed taken where it crossed the same point) plus twice the pace difference; the chance is the zone quality × the class's overtaking factor × a ramp from 1.5% to 8.5% advantage × racecraft × closeness, capped at 80% (lap 1 × 1.3). A success lets the attacker through with a brief boost (up to its top speed) while the defender loses a quarter of a second; the defender cannot strike back for 15% of a lap. A failure costs the attacker 0.1 to 0.35 s and occasionally ends in contact (time lost for both, sometimes a retirement). A car a lap or more ahead passes a backmarker with a 90% chance at any braking zone, and a car of a faster class passes a slower-class car with 85%; neither counts as an overtake.

DRS opens when the gap at the detection point, from each car's crossing times, is within the class's limit from the given lap, on green and on a track less than 30% wet.

Each lap draws at most one incident from the class's rates, scaled by the driver's error rate, by 1.5 on tyres past their life and by the wet-weather risk of the tyres fitted: a technical failure (reliability per km), a crash, a trip off (3 to 10 s, off the racing line so others pass) or a small mistake (0.4 to 1.5 s). A failed car limps back to the pits (35%), parks where the marshals can leave it, or stops where it has to be recovered.

### Race control

Race control follows the fastest class's `flags` rules and draws from its own random stream:

- a trip off, contact or a parked car brings a single yellow (20 to 45 s) from 250 m before the spot to 60 m after it;
- a crash or a car stopped on track brings a double yellow for the recovery (60 to 210 s) and may neutralise the race. A safety car comes out with a chance that grows with the speed at the spot (30% to 70% for a crash; less in series with a full course yellow, which keep it for barrier repairs); otherwise often a virtual safety car or full course yellow until the car is recovered. A series without a virtual safety car (IndyCar, TCR) uses the safety car more; bikes only wave yellows. Nothing is neutralised on the last lap or after the flag;
- the safety car joins 150 m ahead of the leader, in a gap between cars, and runs at 70% of the fastest class's race speed (at most 198 km/h). Cars catch up at the safety car delta and queue behind it. Once the track is clear and it has led a full lap, it is "in this lap" at the line, leaves at the pit entry (or 400 m before the line), and the car behind it leads the field to the line, where racing resumes; nobody passes before crossing it;
- when a VSC or full course yellow ends, or at the restart, every car pulls away from its current speed on the launch curve.

A red flag (`redFlag`) stops the race. It comes after a crash, with a chance growing with the speed at the spot (8% to 30% for cars, 2% to 10% for bikes), or for a car stopped where it must be recovered (2%). Rain also stops a car race once both the rain and the wetness pass 85% (`redFlag.rain` is false for bikes). Not on the last lap.
- Any safety car or VSC ends, and every running car drives at the safety car delta to where its series waits (`redFlag.stopAt`). With `pitlane`, a car joins the queue at the pit exit in the order the cars arrive (8 m apart, closer when the lane is short). A car already in its box finishes its stop and then joins the queue. With `track`, each car has a slot in single file before its next crossing of the line, nearest the line first, and stops there.
- The race waits for the clearing-up time (`clearMinutes`, drawn from race control's stream), or, for rain, until the rain has eased below 50%, then ten minutes more. Where `raceClockStops` is set, the stoppage is added to the time limit (`limit`).
- At the restart each car takes new tyres where the rules allow (`work`): for the conditions, or the first stint of a fresh plan. The safety car leads the field away. The queue leaves the pit lane 1.5 s apart (from on track, the field first and any cars in the pit lane after it). The safety car is in this lap from the start, so it leads one lap.
- A rolling restart then goes as after any safety car. A standing one (Formula 1 in the dry) has the field take grid slots behind the line in race order (`formGrid`), each car driving to its own slot (`regrid`). Once all have stopped, or after two minutes, the lights go out 5 s later and everyone launches from zero after their reaction. Bikes have no safety car: they leave the pit lane for a sighting lap at reduced pace, then form up on the grid the same way, in grid order if the leader had done fewer than three laps.
- A bike race stopped after its share of the distance (`completeAtPct`: 75% MotoGP, 66.7% WorldSBK) is over at once. A race whose time runs out while stopped, or that waits more than an hour, is called off (`callOff`): cars are classified by laps done, then by when they did them, as they stood at the end of the lap `resultLapsBack` before the lap the red flag came out on (as at the red flag itself for WorldSBK).

The pit lane stays open throughout. A neutralised lap makes a stop cheap, so a car that still has to stop before the end (for fuel, tyres, the driver or its plan) takes it when the stop is due soon, the tyres are half worn, half the tank is gone or a driver change is near.

### Tyres, fuel, drivers and stops

A set loses `deg × wear` of lap time up to the end of its life (wear 1), then falls off a cliff: `deg × (1 + 6x + 20x²)` for x past it. Classes without refuelling plan before the start: a dynamic programme over (laps covered, compounds used) per number of stints finds the fastest split over the dry compounds, with stints capped at 1.6 lives, the two-compound rule (lifted once wet-weather tyres have been used) and mandatory stops enforced; the choice is random among plans within a small margin of the best, and the stop lap is spread by about 1.5 laps. At the decision point 500 m before the pit entry a car stops when the plan says so, when its tyres are past 1.08 lives, to cover a rival in its class close behind who has just stopped, to try the undercut after two laps stuck within a second of the car ahead, for a cheap stop under a neutralisation, or for the weather. Classes with refuelling stop when less than 1.2 laps of fuel remain, fill up to what the rest of the race needs, and change tyres when the set would pass 0.9 of its life in the next stint.

The weather call compares the tyres fitted with the best type for the average wetness over the next two and a half laps (what a team sees on the radar). When the time gained over up to eight laps beats the cost of a stop, the car comes in; slicks on a wet track count half as much again, wet tyres overheating on a dry one add to it, and teams weigh the stop differently. A class that does not stop at all (MotoGP, Superbike, TCR) comes in only for the weather, as in flag-to-flag racing. A race that starts wet starts on wet-weather tyres, and the dry plan waits until the track dries.

In a crew, the driver in the car hands over at a stop when their stint would pass the class's longest stint (`driverStintMinutes`) before the next stop, to the team-mate who has driven least. The change happens while refuelling (`driverChangeS`); tyres come afterwards, or at the same time in classes that do both at once.

In the pit lane a car brakes to the limit, drives to its team's box (waiting if a team-mate is still being served), stops for the service time (tyres, fuel at the refuelling rate and the driver change, concurrently or one after the other, the minimum stationary time of a mandatory stop, and a 4% chance of a slow stop), pulls away on the launch curve and rejoins; under a full course yellow the limit holds out to the exit. Its race progress is the lane mapped onto the stretch of lap it bypasses, so timing lines inside the lane still count.

### Timing and the end

Lap and sector times come from line crossings; sector colours compare with the car's and its class's best. Gaps use the timing loops: each car keeps the last two laps of loop times, and a gap is the difference between two cars' times at the loop the rear car passed last. A car a full lap or more behind shows laps instead. The race order is race progress (finishers by finish time within a lap count, retired cars last); the class order is the race order filtered by class, with gaps and intervals to the class leader and to the car ahead in the class.

A race by laps ends when the overall leader completes them, or at the time limit of the fastest class; a race by time at the leader's first crossing after the time. Everyone else, in every class, finishes at their next crossing, so slower classes run fewer laps; their fuel and strategy are planned for the laps they will actually get.

### The race weekend

`weekend.ts` runs the practice sessions and qualifying stages before the race on one field, to each series' format (the `weekend` group in `data/racing.json`, read by `parseWeekend`; the figures and their sources come from the 2026 sporting regulations).
- **Sessions in order.** Practice is shared by every class, to the fastest class's schedule. Qualifying runs class by class, the slowest first, each stage a session of its own.
- **Who runs in a stage.** A stage takes its cars from the results so far (`stageCars`): every car of the class; the top or the rest of an earlier stage; or odd or even places (groups). "practice" is the best laps over the practice sessions listed, or over the lap-time model when none ran.
- **Knockout counts.** In a knockout format (`knockout`) the counts are rescaled so as many cars go out at each step whatever the field size, the last stage keeping its size.
- **The grid** (`grid`). A stage's round is one more than the latest round it draws on, so stages of one round ran side by side. Each car is placed by the latest round it reached, then by its place there; groups of one round take places in turn. With `averageTimes`, cars are ordered by how many stages they set a time in, then by the mean of their best laps.
- **Rubber.** It starts at 0.25 (what support races leave). It keeps 92% from one session to the next on the same day, all of it between stages of qualifying, and 70% overnight.
- **Tyre knowledge.** Each team's belief about its tyre wear is off by an error drawn once per team and compound (a normal draw times 20%). The error shrinks by the square root of 1 + laps / 3, counting the laps the team saw at race pace: long-run laps fully, push laps a quarter. The race's strategy (`planStrategy`, `pickCompound`) plans with the believed wear (`wearGuess`); the tyres wear as they really do, and a car still comes in when its tyres pass 1.08 lives.

`SessionSim` (in `session.ts`) is a `RaceSim` with a `session` spec in its setup. It overrides the race's hooks (`lapKind`, `afterLap`, `decide`, `planService`, `finishService`, `paceLoss`, `lapFactor`, `incidentRisk`, `drsAllowed`, `yields`, `passed`, `trackIncident`, `updateOrder` and the gaps), so the track, pit lane, weather, incidents and timing are the race's own.
- **Garages and runs.**
  - Every car taking part starts in a garage of its own along the boxes; the others are left out (`finished`, off the map). Between runs a car stands stopped in its box, pushed back into the garage (`garage` 0 to 1 over 2.5 s, which `pose` turns into a place inside the open garage, facing the lane), until the session clock reaches the time its next run starts.
  - A run is the out lap from the pit exit, a list of laps (push, cool-down, long run), then an in lap: `goIn` sets a pit request at the line after the last lap, and the car comes into the garage.
  - Lap factors: out lap 1.15 (1.3 in qualifying), cool-down 1.28, in lap 1.2.
  - A push lap in qualifying gives nothing away to race pace, and its segment times are scaled so the lap with the wing open in every DRS zone equals the analysis's qualifying lap (`pushScale`); in practice it gives away half the class's race pace. A long run is a race lap on race fuel.
  - Only push, cool-down and long-run laps that began at the line on track are timed, and only their sectors count towards the best. A lap ending in the pit lane is an in lap. DRS is free in every zone.
- **Planning runs.**
  - Practice: an installation run within the first five minutes, then short runs (2 to 4 push laps with cool-down laps between, low fuel, the softest slick or a race compound). In a session with `longRuns` there is a race simulation of 8 to 15 laps on a race compound (team-mates on different ones). 3 to 10 minutes pass in the garage between runs, twice that in the wet.
  - Qualifying: one, two or three runs by the session's length. A run is 1 push lap, push-cool-push, or three in a row for a single-run session. The last run leaves so its final push lap begins 10 to 60 s before the flag. A car well through to the next stage (`advance`) skips its last run six times in ten.
- **Traffic.**
  - A car on a push lap that comes within 0.6 s of a car on a slow lap is waved by (`waveBy`): it goes by losing 0.15 s, now and then 0.4 to 1.6 s (a spoilt lap), and the slow car lifts.
  - At braking zones everyone in a session yields like a backmarker, so nobody races for position. Passes are no news.
- **The clock and the flag.** The session clock runs with the session and stops under a red flag where the rules say so. When it runs out, the chequered flag: a car's next crossing of the line takes the flag, so a lap begun before it counts, and the car comes in. The session is over when everyone is back in the garage or out of it.
- **Red flags in a session.** A crash brings a red flag with an 85% chance, a stopped car with 60%. Every running car's lap becomes an in lap, the pit exit closes, and after 4 to 12 minutes it reopens and every team plans its next run in the time left. In qualifying, the car that caused it loses lap times by `qualifyingPenalty`.
- **Order.** By best lap, the earlier time first, cars without a time after. Gaps and intervals are between best laps. The session reports, per car, its best lap, timed laps and the laps it saw on each compound (`seen`).

### Recording, telemetry and statistics

Besides timing, the simulation records per car and lap:

- a lap record: time, sectors, overall and class position, the race time at the line and the gap to the first car of the class to complete that lap, compound, tyre age and wear, fuel, whether the car stopped, its speed through the speed trap (timed over the last 20 m before the trap line), the driver, the track wetness and whether the lap was neutralised;
- a telemetry trace: the time since the start of the lap at every sample station (every 5 m, coarser on laps over 5 km so a lap keeps at most about 1000 samples; a Float32Array per lap);
- every pit stop: entry and exit times, time in the box (including waiting for a team-mate), tyres before and after, fuel, the driver who took over and the reason.

For the race it records every neutralisation (kind, start, end, reason), the yellow zones, and every half minute the rain, wetness, flag state and how many cars run on each tyre type.

`telemetry.ts` turns a trace into channels. Speed is racing-line distance over time between the neighbouring samples; longitudinal acceleration is the change in speed over two samples either side; lateral acceleration is v² times the line's curvature at the sample; the gear comes from the gearing; throttle and brake come from the force balance of the lap simulation (mass with the fuel on board, drag and downforce at the class's trim, rolling resistance, gradient). The throttle is the share of power needed; the brake is the share of the grip-limited braking force, with the grip the fitted tyres have at that lap's wetness. A complete lap ends with a sample at the line at the lap time, so a delta between two laps (the difference of their sample times) ends at the lap-time difference.

`stats.ts` reads the rankings and chart series from these records and the event log, for one class or class by class: fastest laps, best trap speeds (laps without a stop), overtakes made and lost (from 'overtake' events, so lapping and passing slower classes are not counted), pit stops, positions (overall or in the class) and gaps per lap, tyre stints (a new stint where the compound changes or the tyre age drops back to one lap), driver stints, and the neutralised periods as leader laps. `export.ts` writes results, laps, the feed and telemetry as RFC 4180 CSV.

### In the app

`RaceController` builds a model per class from the store's current track, analysis and facilities and a `Weekend` from the settings (with the sessions left out, `skip`), then opens the sessions one after another and the race last. **Next** simulates the rest of the session on now and opens the next; **Skip to race** runs every session left in 12 ms slices without showing them (a whole Formula 1 weekend in about a second), recording each in the weekend as it ends. The race waits on the grid for the broadcast (`holdStart`); sessions start straight away. It plays a session or the race back with requestAnimationFrame (simulated time = real time × speed up to 1000×, at most 12 ms of stepping per frame, positions interpolated between the last two steps), and skips to the end in 12 ms slices. It stops the race when the track or its analysis changes. A Formula 1 race runs to the end in about a quarter of a second, a six-hour race with 54 cars in three classes in about three seconds. Structural changes go out as the store's `race` topic; per-frame updates reach the map, the tower, the panel and the dock through `onTick`, and they refresh a few times a second. The controller also holds the class shown in a multi-class race, which the tower's tabs and the dock's class picker share.

The map draws the cars in team colours (ringed in their class colour in a multi-class race), the safety car on top of its queue, yellow zones along the track, a dashed yellow lap under a VSC or full course yellow, a wet sheen on the track as it gets wet, and rain over the map.

In a session the panel shows the session's clock and flags, the selected car's run (in the garage and when it goes out, out lap, push lap and so on), a classification refreshed every second (with the cut line in qualifying), and the weekend's sessions with the fastest of each. The tower orders by best lap, shows the time left and each car's state (GAR, OUT, IN, COOL, RUN, PIT), and in qualifying tints the cars below the cut. The 3D view leaves out its own aside for a stopped car in a session, since the simulation's pose already puts it in the garage, and the replay buffer records how far each car stands in its garage.

`RaceDock` replaces the profile strip during a race. The controller holds the telemetry selection (car, lap, comparison), which the dock's selectors and the telemetry export share; picking a car on the map or in the tower sets it and opens the Telemetry tab. The lap charts shade neutralised laps and red flags; in a session the position and gap charts are gone and lap times show timed laps only (out and in laps leave a gap); Conditions plots rain, wetness, flags and the field's tyres against race time. The dock redraws only when the recorded data moves on (a lap completed, a stop, a new selection or class; every second for the lap in progress and the conditions) or on hover. Telemetry hover sets the store's hover station, so the map marks the spot; chart clicks select the car under the pointer.
